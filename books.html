Start with a clear purpose

Before asking for funding, write down exactly what the money is for and what it should produce. Lenders fund a purpose they can understand — equipment, inventory, working capital, a location — not a general hope that cash will help.

Keep records organized

Bank statements, tax records, and bookkeeping that agree with each other make a business easier to review. Organized records do not guarantee anything, but messy records slow every conversation down.

Separate personal and business credit

Use business accounts for business activity and keep personal spending out of them. A clean separation helps a reviewer see the business on its own — and helps you see it clearly too.

Recommended reading

Recommended reading

Profit First

by Mike Michalowicz

A cash-management system for business owners: when money comes in, move a set percentage to profit first, then run the business on what remains, using separate accounts for profit, owner's pay, taxes, and operating expenses. The point is discipline by design — profit stops being whatever happens to be left over at the end of the month.

Recommended reading

The E-Myth Revisited

by Michael E. Gerber

Gerber's classic argues that most small businesses struggle not from a lack of skill but because owners spend all their time working in the business instead of on it. His answer is to build systems — documented ways of doing the work that produce the same result every time — so the business can run well without depending on the owner being present for every task.
